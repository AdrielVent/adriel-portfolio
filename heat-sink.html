Heat sink for a 100 W CPU

I designed a forced-convection heat sink to keep a 50.8 mm CPU at or below 85 °C. My model predicts 69.85 °C. Nothing has been built or bench tested yet, and this page says where the prediction could be wrong.

Problem and constraints

A desktop CPU's existing cooler was inadequate. The replacement had to remove 100 W at steady state from a vertically mounted 50.8 × 50.8 mm chip in 25 °C case air, and bolt to the existing mounting hardware. The 85 °C limit was the course requirement; I added a 70 °C target to force margin into the design.

Each temperature limit sets a maximum total thermal resistance from chip to air:

Rmax = (Tmax − T∞) / Q 85 °C limit: (85 − 25) / 100 = 0.600 °C/W. 70 °C target: (70 − 25) / 100 = 0.450 °C/W.

My role

This was an individual course project. The calculations, MATLAB model, CAD, Fusion 360 study, drawings and report are mine; the report carries a signed declaration of original work. The problem statement and the 85 °C requirement came from the course.

I also 3D-printed plastic models of the 17-fin and 15-fin designs to compare fin spacing and tool access in the design review. They say nothing about thermal performance.

Approach and assumptions

I modeled the heat path as resistances in series, from chip to air:

  1. CPU100 W
  2. Contact / thermal pad0 in the base case
  3. Al-6061 base0.0023 °C/W
  4. 15 fins to air0.4462 °C/W
  5. Ambient air25 °C

Two inputs are assumptions, and both matter more than any geometry choice:

  • Convection coefficient h = 65 W/m²K. Not backed by fan-curve, pressure-drop or CFD data.
  • Zero contact resistance between chip and base in the headline number. Section 7 shows what a real pad does.

Decisions and tradeoffs

I screened three concepts on thermal performance, manufacturability, airflow, tool access, mass, cost and how ready each was to prototype.

Concept screening
ConceptWhyDecision
Copper pin-fin, natural convectionHeavy; natural convection too weak for 100 W in this footprintRejected
Aluminum folded-fin with heat pipesWould work, but adds cost, interfaces and packaging riskRejected
Al-6061 plate-fin with a 50 mm fanBest balance of performance, cost and machinabilitySelected

Inside the plate-fin concept, I first modeled an idealized 17-fin baseline, then traded it for 15 fins. The 17-fin version ran 8.3 °C cooler in Fusion 360, but its 2.11 mm gaps are hard to machine and reach with tools. Fifteen fins open the gap to 2.557 mm and cut the mass 27.5% (75.3 g to 54.6 g), while still meeting 85 °C with margin.

Simulation and testing

I ran steady-state thermal studies of both fin counts in Fusion 360 with the same heat load, ambient and convection inputs. These are simplified predictions, not validation.

Not tested yet. I wrote a bench-test plan but have not run it: a 100 W cartridge heater in a 50.8 mm block standing in for the CPU, the fan at rated voltage, and Type-K thermocouples at the interface, base, fin, inlet and outlet, logged until steady state. The test equipment is budgeted at $376.92 if bought new.

Results and limitations

Both models predict a pass on the 85 °C requirement. The 70 °C target is met by 0.15 °C, and only with zero contact resistance. 69.85 °C is the MATLAB chip temperature; 69.775 °C is the hottest point in the Fusion model. They measure different things, so their closeness is not a validation.

Limitations

  • The convection coefficient is assumed, and it decides pass or fail (Figure 3).
  • 0.0031 °C/W of contact resistance, the high end of the ideal range I used for a PTM7950 pad, erases the 0.15 °C margin.
  • The Fusion 360 result depends on which faces get convection, the mesh and the contact settings. I have not shown a mesh convergence check.
  • The mass is a MATLAB estimate; the CAD mass should replace it.
  • The drawing is not yet a fabrication drawing (Figure 6).

What I'd change next

  1. Measure the fan's airflow and the pressure drop through the fins, so h is a measured input instead of an assumption.
  2. Run the 100 W bench test, reading interface temperature first, since the interface decides the 70 °C target.
  3. Replace the MATLAB mass with the CAD mass, and fill in the drawing's material, tolerances and title block.
  4. Add a mesh convergence check to the Fusion 360 study.
