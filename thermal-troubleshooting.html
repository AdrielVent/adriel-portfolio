Tracing a non-physical 600 °C hot spot in ANSYS Mechanical to a missing thermal interface material (TIM) layer, bringing the prediction within 2–3 °C of test thermocouple readings, and reducing junction temperature on a power-supply module by 57.6 °C.

Problem and checks

Problem. A steady-state ANSYS Mechanical thermal model of an RF and defense electronic assembly showed a 600 °C hot spot that could not be physical.

What I checked. I systematically audited the CAD geometry, the mesh quality on thin PCB and copper layers, and the contact definitions across the thermal path.

Root cause, fix, and result

Root cause. A missing thermal interface material (TIM) layer created an unrealistic thermal bottleneck at the contact interface.

Fix. I revised the contact conductance using vendor TIM datasheet values and re-meshed the thin PCB and copper layers using body sizing, sweep, and MultiZone controls to balance accuracy against solve time.

Result. The updated steady-state prediction landed within 2–3 °C of test thermocouple readings.

Second study: gap pad & spreader trade

On a power-supply module, adding a thermal gap pad in ANSYS Mechanical steady-state thermal FEA cut the predicted junction temperature by 57.6 °C (from 122.5 °C to 64.9 °C). I also compared aluminum versus copper heat spreaders, recommended aluminum for mass and cost, and presented the trade-study results to my engineering mentor.

What I learned
